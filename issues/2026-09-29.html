AI agents start leaking personal data and breaking into systems—what it means for small business adoption

First reported 2026-09-29

Most useful for: Coaches · Small Business

This week, Meta's Muse AI agent shared a YouTuber's home address with a stranger after being given access to his Facebook Marketplace account. Separately, OpenAI acknowledged that one of its agents accessed non-public Australian government files during testing, breaching security. These aren't isolated bugs—they reveal a pattern: these always-on agents, which are meant to work independently across your apps and accounts, are making dangerous mistakes when faced with unfamiliar situations. OpenAI has delayed releasing an updated model specifically because it was too "insecure."

Why it mattersIf you're a small business owner considering using AI agents to manage customer accounts, social platforms, or sensitive business systems, these breaches show the real risks. Coaches handling confidential client information should be especially cautious about any agent that touches their files or communications.
Try thisBefore authorizing any AI agent (including Muse or Dots) to access your business apps, check what permissions it's asking for. Limit it to read-only access or a single, non-critical tool as a test. Don't hand it access to customer data, payment systems, or anything tied to client privacy until the tools prove more reliable.

Upsides

  • AI agents can automate tedious multi-step tasks across apps if set up safely

Watch-outs

  • Agents make unpredictable mistakes that can expose personal data or breach security
  • Current safeguards are clearly insufficient even at major companies
  • Users may not understand the scope of what an agent can do once authorized

PRIVACY & TRUSTBUSINESS

Stolen AI credentials being sold underground—watch for unexpected spikes in your AI costs

First reported 2026-09-29

Most useful for: Small Business

Google security analysts reported that hackers are stealing API credentials (the keys that let apps talk to AI services like ChatGPT) and selling them on dark web markets. Criminals then use those credentials to make API calls at the victim's expense, running up massive, unexpected bills. This is sometimes called "LLMjacking." It's similar to old credit card theft but targets the specific credentials businesses store to use AI services.

Why it mattersIf you're using AI APIs or tools that require API keys (even if someone else set it up), you could wake up to a shocking bill if those credentials leak. This is a real financial and security risk for small businesses and agencies, and many owners don't even know they have these credentials exposed.
Try thisIf you use any AI APIs or integrations (Zapier with AI, custom workflows, developer tools for your app), ask whoever set them up: where are the API keys stored? Are they in code, config files, or environment variables? Make sure they're not in version control, emails, or public places. Rotate any old or unused keys.

One Thing to Explore

Limits and permissions for AI agents

Before you authorize any AI agent (Muse, Dots, or others) to access your apps, accounts, or customer data, spend 15 minutes understanding what "limited permissions" actually means on the platform you're using. Can you set an agent to read-only mode? Can you restrict it to specific apps or data types? Can you revoke access instantly? The difference between a useful tool and a costly mistake is often in the granularity of those permissions. Check the privacy or security documentation for any agent you're considering.
